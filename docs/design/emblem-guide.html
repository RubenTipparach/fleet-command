Emblem Design Guide

How Fleet Command's faction emblems are designed, the rules they follow, and the record of your notes on them. An emblem says what a people believes, so every shape in it is chosen for a reason, and the reason is written beside it. The Concord comes first: four directions, sketched flat, waiting for your pick before anything is modelled.

Your notes

In your words, newest first, with what each one changes. They are the rules for every emblem from here on.

    The method

    1. Step 1Start from the peopleRead the faction's world view, bodies, technology and ships (the console skins design). List what the people believe in a few short lines.
    2. Step 2Sketch flat, in SVGSeveral different directions. Each one is a single image with labels pointing at every element and saying why it is there. No circle-and-dots, nothing a primitive could make on its own.
    3. Step 3You pickLeave a note or pick one below. Nothing goes to 3D until a shape is chosen: the shape matters first.
    4. Step 4Sculpt it in 3DBlender, built from swept and cut forms, never primitives placed on a disc; surfaces from Material Maker graphs (metal, enamel, glass); lit like a cast badge.
    5. Step 5Check it smallThe console crest at 96 px, the marks at 40 px and 20 px, and on a hull. If it blurs at 20 px, the shape changes, not the size.

    What the reference badges teach

    From the empire badges on the reference game's box (shared 2026-11-03). What we take is how they are built and lit, in our own words. None of their symbols, shapes or art comes into Fleet Command (CLAUDE.md section 1).

    Every outline is its own. Wings, blades, a horned crescent, an oval: almost none is a plain circle, and you can name the empire from the outline alone at the size of a thumbnail.
    They are objects, not drawings. Each badge is cast metal standing off the surface, with a soft shadow falling to the lower right, as if pinned to a uniform.
    A metal frame round coloured inlay. Polished chrome or gold holds enamel or glass of one or two colours. The metal carries the outline; the colour fills the shape inside it.
    Bevels catch one light. A hard highlight runs along every edge turned toward the upper left, and every edge turned away goes dark. That is what makes the outline read.
    Relief has texture. Wings have engraved feathers, fields have specks of stars, gems have facets. Nothing is a smooth plain primitive.
    Depth in layers. A sunken field, a raised device, the frame highest. The eye reads three heights even at small size.
    Open frames. Several are cut through, so the background shows inside the badge, which makes them lighter and more distinct than a solid disc.
    Each says one idea. A bird of prey, a blade, a sun, an eye: one picture per people, never a collage.

    What the Visual Standards Manual teaches

    A fan organisation's rules for its own marks (STARFLEET International, 2026; link under References). It is about governing a mark rather than drawing one, which is what Fleet Command's emblems need next. Printed page numbers in brackets. We take its rules, never its marks, names or art.

    Every element is written down with its meaning (p. 4-5). The background, the centre symbol and the wreath each have a stated meaning. Ours: every callout on a sketch.
    A flat version beside the full one (p. 5). A no-gradient version exists for patches and coins. Ours: the 3D render for the console and a flat version for paint, patches and 20 px.
    A named palette, with flat stand-ins (p. 6-7). Each colour has a name and a use, plus solid colours for goods that cannot carry a gradient. Ours: each emblem's colours named from its faction's palette, each with a flat paint colour.
    Clear space round the mark (p. 8). A fixed margin that nothing may enter. Ours: rule 9 below.
    A solid dark ground, and one-colour use (p. 9). Shown on plain dark colours; in one colour it takes one dark ink. Ours: the one-colour mark, which also takes the state colour at 20 px.
    Never stretched (p. 10). Resized only at its own proportions.
    A family system (p. 11-12). Regions, auxiliaries and committees share one frame (a border, a ring, a wreath) and each designs its own interior; the metal of the border, text and wreath (gold or silver) says what kind of body it is. Ours, later: a faction's fleets, houses or clans could share its frame with their own interior (survey E1).
    Status is added, never redrawn (p. 13). A flagship gains a banner with laurels and stars; after its service the border is extended to show it once held the role. Ours: honours and tallies (a Krai victory, a Ssarith kill) as additions to the emblem, never a new one.
    Starships only when abstracted (p. 14). An identifiable starship is not allowed; only one abstracted into an original mark. This weighs against Concord sketch B.
    Keep well clear of protected shapes (p. 15-16). Any arrowhead-delta form is avoided, and where a design comes near one, its contour, proportions, colour and inner symbol are all changed. Avoiding the reference altogether is the safest course. Ours: rule 15.

    The rejected Concord ring (a field of stars inside a round border) is close to this manual's own frame, a ring round a starfield. That is one more reason it is gone.

    The rules for every emblem

    Compiled from your notes, the reference badges and the manual. Where a rule comes from is in brackets.

      Concord

      What the Concord believes (console skins design, provisional until you correct it): a federation of many species that decides by consent; it explores, mediates and defends. Its people have every shape of hand, claw and tentacle. Its technology is clean light behind glass. Its ships are saucers and slender nacelles in long smooth sweeps, and its console frames change thickness at every turn. Its colours: Signal blue for trust and the flag, Sky for clarity, Accord green for consent given, Lilac for the many species mixed, Deep blue for the night sky every homeworld shares.

      Rejected: the ring of five worlds (v1, 3D)

      A platinum ring round five glass domes and an open hub, rendered before any sketch was agreed.

      Your words: "I'm not a fan of the circle design here", "its too simple and easy to make, see our rules about just slapping on primitives", "this is bad design". Every rule under Your notes comes from this.
