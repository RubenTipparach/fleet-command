Ship Design Bible

How a Fleet Command ship is designed, worked on the Concord line. A ship is built from the inside out: the rooms first, then the hull wrapped round them, then the parts that make its class, and only then the skin, windows, lights, markings and damage. Every faction follows the method; each changes the shapes.

Chapter 1

The method: inside out

A hull drawn first is a shape with nothing in it: it reads as a model, and its windows, doors and damage have nowhere true to go. A hull drawn round its rooms reads as a ship, because everything on its skin is the outside of something inside. Star Crew Bridge Simulator designs its ships this way (its federation-ship-concepts change); Fleet Command does too.

  1. Rooms. List what the ship does and who is aboard; turn that into rooms as blocks, deck by deck (chapter 2).
  2. Hull. Wrap the blocks in a lofted hull with a measured clearance; the hull's stations come out of the blocks, not out of a sketch (chapter 3).
  3. Parts. Split the ship into its named parts: primary hull, secondary hull, neck, bridge block, struts, nacelles, drive (chapters 4 to 6).
  4. Skin. Windows only where a room meets the hull (7), rooms seen through them (8), lights that graze the plating (9), markings and livery (10).
  5. Material. The exterior complements the faction's console without sharing its materials or colours (11).
  6. Damage. What breaks shows the decks and rooms behind it (12).
M1Never draw a hull without the rooms it holds. A change to the hull starts as a change to a room.
M2Every number is in metres and comes from data (data/ships/), read by the page and later by the Blender generator: one source.
Chapter 2

Floor plans from blocks

A room is a box: its width across the ship, its length along it, and the decks it spans. Decks are 3.5 m apart with 2.8 m of headroom; corridors are 2.4 m wide down the centreline; doors are 1.2 by 2.1 m. The worked example is the Petrel frigate, 42 crew, three decks (data/ships/concord/petrel_plan.json). The plans below are drawn from that file.

CommandBridge, ready roomTop deck, forward: the bridge looks ahead and is the hardest place to reach
CrewQuarters, mess, medbay, officersOutboard, mid-ship: they get the windows
SystemsComputer, sensors, life support, damage control, shields, switchboardInboard or where their emitter must reach the skin
WeaponsTorpedo room, magazineForward and low, near their launchers
EngineeringEngineering, reactorAft and low, on the drive's axis; the reactor deepest
BayShuttle bayLow, with a door to the skin
CorridorOne spine a deckCentreline, 2.4 m wide, joining every room on its deck
P1No two rooms on a deck overlap; every room touches its deck's corridor. The page checks both:
P2Rooms that want daylight (crew, command) go outboard; rooms that want armour (reactor, magazine) go inboard and low.
Chapter 3

Enclose them in the hull

The hull is fitted, not drawn. Every 2 m along the ship, the page takes the rooms at that station and sets the hull's half beam, top and bottom to clear them by 1.05 m: 0.45 m of air between room and inner skin (Star Crew's clearance) plus 0.6 m of frames and plating. The corners are chamfered only as far as the rooms allow. The bow runs 6 m past the first room and tapers; the stern ends 3 m past the last, square, where the drive is. Then every room corner is measured against the hull.

Rooms coloured by kind, the fitted hull as a shell, and the Petrel as mockup 6 builds it today for comparison. Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom.

H1Every room corner lies at least 1.05 m inside the hull. A room that fails moves, or the hull grows: never both by eye.
H2The fitted hull is the minimum. A class's character is added on top (a longer bow, a swept stern, a raised command block), never by cutting into a room.
H3Measure the as-built hull against its rooms the same way. Mockup 6's Petrel was drawn before its rooms: the comparison above shows where it is too tight or too loose, and its stations get refitted from the plan.

Chapter 5

Does the bridge need a shape?

Owner, survey A18, 2026-11-03"larger super captial ships, otherwise it depends based on human feedback"

On the largest ships, yes. A super-capital (the Condor and up) gives its bridge a block of its own: at that size the bridge is a target worth picking out, and the bracket, the bridge fire and the crown blown off all land on it. On every other class the bridge stays inside the hull's outline, a room like the others, until players' feedback says a class needs one. Toggle the block in the figure above to compare.

B1A super-capital's bridge is a raised block on the dorsal centreline, forward of mid-ship, standing at least one deck proud of the hull.
B2It is glazed: a lit band forward and along its sides, the only windows on the ship wider than a person.
B3Smaller classes keep the bridge inside the hull, its windows the only sign; a block is added to a class only when playtests show players need it.
B4Each faction shapes its block its own way (the Concord's is a chamfered block; another faction's a crest, a dome or a beak).
Chapter 6

Chapter 7

Windows

Windows are a must: they are the only thing on a hull that says how big it is. Every faction's windows are the same size, a person's (1.1 m wide, 0.6 m tall), so a player who knows one ship knows the size of every ship.

W1A window only where a room with people in it meets the hull. Never on a corridor's end, a reactor, a magazine, a bay, a stripe or the service band.
W2One row per deck, 1.5 m above the floor, so the rows are 3.5 m apart: count the rows and you count the decks.
W3Windows run in groups of 4 to 12 (a room) with gaps (frames and bulkheads); about one in five is dark.
W4Each window is a real opening onto its room (chapter 8), lit warm or cool by the room, and goes dark with the room's power.
W5The bridge block's glazing is the one exception to the person-sized rule (chapter 5).

The fitted hull in chapter 3 places its windows by these rules from the plan: turn on Windows there to see them follow the crew rooms and stop at engineering.

Chapter 8

Parallax interiors

Behind every window is a room, drawn without a single extra triangle: the hull's shader casts a ray from the eye through the glass into a box the size of the room behind it and paints the floor, ceiling, walls and furniture it hits (interior mapping). The room shifts with the viewpoint as a real room would.

  • The box is the room's own size, taken from the plan: 3.5 m a deck, the room's width and length. A window on a 6 m cabin shows a 6 m cabin.
  • Each kind of room has its own furnishing: quarters (bunks, carpet, a plant), mess (tables), medbay (beds, white light), engineering (consoles, warm light), bridge (stations, a viewscreen).
  • Light follows power: a room whose section loses power goes dark or to red emergency light, and its windows with it.
  • Cost: one shader on the hull material, no geometry. On a phone, rooms beyond 600 m fall back to a flat lit pane.

L1Lamps stand off the hull and tilt into it; a light respects the surface's facing (a face turned away from it gets none), and the hull casts shadows.

Chapter 11

Exterior: complement the console

Owner, 2026-11-03"ship exterior material should compliment the UI, not share materials or colors directly. Ship exterior depending on the faction should focus on distinct features, color scheme and unique paneling"

A faction's console and its ships come from the same people, so they belong together, but they are seen at different sizes and for different reasons. The console is read up close, under the captain's hand. The ship is read across a battle, against space and against other ships. So a ship's exterior is designed to sit beside its faction's console as a partner: the same ideology said a second way, never the console's surface pasted on a hull.

X1Complement, as a guide, not a hard rule (owner, 2026-11-03: "I said compliment, so not a hard rule"). A hull's material and palette are chosen to sit well beside its console: they may share a colour or a material where that helps the faction read as one people, and nothing measures the distance between them.
X3Every faction's exterior is set apart by three things, and needs all three: distinct features (the parts and shapes only it has: its bridge, its nacelles or their absence, its prow), its colour scheme (field colour, stripe colour, light colour) and unique paneling (how its plates are cut and laid: the Concord's straight strakes and ring bands; another faction's plates are cut another way).
X4Test it side by side: the faction's console skin and its ship on one screen read as one people, and neither looks like a piece of the other.
FactionConsole (stays there)Ship exterior (its own)
ConcordFlat back-lit vector panels, blue and lilac on blackPearl armour, blue bands , straight panel strakes, warm-lit windows
Kethran HegemonyArmoured gunmetal plates, bolts, plain wedge keysTo design: its own field colour and plate cut, complementing the gunmetal

Chapter 12

Parallax damage

Damage is read by eye, and what it opens shows the rooms behind it, by the same interior mapping as the windows (chapter 8).

HitWhat shows
PhaserA scorch mark that glows hot and cools; no hole
TorpedoA hole whose depth shows the deck and room behind it, lit by the room's light or by fire, rimmed with char and a cooling ember line
A part breaks offThe broken faces show the decks and bulkheads at their real heights; the pieces burn along their edges and trail flame
Subsystem signaturesEach system fails in its own colour and place: nacelles flicker then vent violet plasma, the reactor leaks white-cyan coolant then breaches, the bridge smokes then burns, weapons spark
D1A hole opens onto the room the plan puts there: a torpedo through the crew quarters shows bunks, through engineering shows machinery.
D2Colours keep one meaning: orange fire, cyan-white coolant, violet plasma, sparks for power.
Chapter 13

Culture, not anatomy

Owner, 2026-11-04"you are over stereotyping an alien species which is extremely insensitive. We've stated multiple times before, the look of an alien species is not their UI, not their spaceship. Cultural ideologies are far more deep and complex than what someone's skin or features are. And that no culture in the world follows a single line of thinking when it comes to desigining UI, space ships, logos, dressing etc, and none of those reflect the way they look"

A ship is made by a people, not grown from their bodies. People who look alike build very different things, and one people builds in many ways at once: its shipyards, eras, regions, classes and schools of engineering disagree. So a faction's ships come from what its people think, remember, value, trade and know how to make, and from the arguments between them, never from what the species looks like.

C1Never from the body. No claws because the makers have claws; no scales, hide, fur or feathers on a hull because they have them; no beak, jaw, eye or limb shapes because they have those. What a people looks like is not what it builds, flies, wears or draws.
C2Many traditions, never one line. A culture holds several design traditions at once (yards, eras, regions, classes, guilds, schools that disagree). A faction's line shows more than one of them, and each class says which tradition built it. One trait never becomes a whole design language.
C3Name the source. Every design choice names where in the culture it comes from (an idea, a history, an institution, an economy, a material or a technology they have) and why that gives this shape. "Because they are that kind of creature" is never a source.
C4The culture is the owner's. The owner sets a faction's culture (survey U7). Where it is too thin to design from, ask before drawing: offer traditions to choose from, never decide them.
C5The stereotype test. Describe the design without naming the species. If the description is a picture of the species' body (a lizard, a bird, an insect, a squid), it fails and is redrawn.

The same holds for a faction's console, emblem, clothing and rooms (CLAUDE.md section 1, "A species' looks are not its design").

Read from the body (wrong)Read from the culture (the method)
They have claws, so the ship has clawsWhat the ship's work is, as its makers see it, and which yard's way of doing that work it follows
Their skin is scaled, so the plating is scalesHow the plating is cut and laid by the people who build it: their materials, tools, trade and history (X3's unique paneling)
One trait (hunting) shapes the whole lineSeveral traditions side by side: an old yard and a new one, a working ship and a ceremonial one, a school that disagrees

Withdrawn on this chapter: the first Ssarith Hunt ships (mockup 12, version 1, 2026-11-04), which took claws, scales and a reptile's sprawl from the species' body.

Chapter 14

Other factions

The Krai ships (mockup 7) were drawn hull first and painted in their console's own metal and colours, which is why they did not read: they are to be redesigned on this method, rooms first, with their culture setting the shapes and an exterior of their own (chapter 11).

Chapter 15

Before you call it done

  1. The plan is data, rooms do not overlap, every room meets its corridor (P1).
  2. The hull is fitted from the plan and every room clears it by 1.05 m (H1), measured on the page.
  3. Every part is named; struts root in structure; nacelles clear every hull by 3 m.
  4. A super-capital's bridge has its block; a smaller class's bridge is inside the hull (B1, B3).
  5. Windows sit only on lived-in rooms, one row a deck (W1, W2).
  6. Lights: each with a job, no overlapping pools, shadows on (L1, L2).
  7. Markings on plain plating, red only for danger.
  8. The hull complements the faction's console (X1, a guide), its features, colours and paneling are its own (X3), and the two are looked at side by side (X4).
  9. Every design choice names its source in the culture (C3), the line shows more than one tradition (C2), and the design passes the stereotype test (C5): nothing is taken from the species' body (C1).
  10. A torpedo through each room kind shows that room (D1).
  11. At 40 px, top and side, the class reads.
  12. Triangles inside the class's budget (survey A1, T3).
